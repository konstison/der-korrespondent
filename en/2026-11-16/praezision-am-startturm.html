Precision at the launch tower: The arduous path to full reusability

SpaceX succeeds in mechanically catching the Starship upper stage for the first time, whilst the loss of the booster underscores the fragility of the system.

Edition of November 16, 2026 · Fact-checked against 4 sources · Deutsche Fassung
In brief
  • On its 15th test flight, SpaceX successfully captured the 52-metre Starship upper stage for the first time, using the mechanical arms of the launch tower at Boca Chica.
  • The anticipated double-catch recovery eluded the company, however, as the Super Heavy booster was deliberately detonated in the sea following a propellant shortfall and a course deviation.
  • This partial success steadies NASA’s ambitious schedule for the Artemis programme, yet simultaneously lays bare persistent developmental pressure amid competition with China.

In the history of spaceflight, watershed moments occur whenever the fundamental architecture of transport undergoes a transformation. Since the dawn of orbital rocketry, the exploration of low-Earth orbit has rested upon a principle of controlled profligacy: colossal multi-stage vehicles burned thousands of tonnes of propellant in a matter of minutes, discarded their engines and tanks over the oceans, and ultimately yielded a diminutive capsule that parachuted back to Earth. What was accepted for decades as the inevitable military and scientific cost of access to the cosmos proved an insurmountable barrier to any broader commercial exploitation. With the fifteenth test flight of its Starship system, the American aerospace firm SpaceX has once again challenged this convention. The successful return of the rocket’s upper stage to the launch tower in Boca Chica, Texas, is undeniably an engineering first. Yet the event demands a sober assessment stripped of technological euphoria and corporate bravado, for the dual recovery that was sought for the first time ultimately proved elusive.

I. The mechanics of the catch: A paradigm shift in spaceflight

On Friday afternoon at 4:47 pm local time (19:47 GMT), the most powerful rocket in spaceflight history, standing 124 metres tall, lifted off from the Starbase launch pad in southern Texas. The sheer scale of the two-stage vehicle—comprising the Super Heavy first-stage booster and the 52-metre Starship upper stage—illustrates the ambition of the endeavour. Following a textbook stage separation, the vehicle embarked upon a mission conceived to stretch far beyond previous tests. For the first time, the plan called not only for the booster to return to the pad, but for the spacecraft itself to be caught directly at the launch tower following an orbital flight. Whereas earlier generations of spaceflight placed their faith in the classical The Space Shuttle paradigm, relying on aerodynamic lift and sprawling runways, SpaceX is pursuing a radically vertical doctrine.

The upper stage reached Earth orbit following its ascent—a milestone achieved by the system only once before, in September last year. During the 22.5-hour mission, the spacecraft circled the globe 14 times. In doing so, it demonstrated not merely its manoeuvrability in a vacuum, but also completed a commercial task: on board were 26 third-generation Starlink satellites, which were successfully deployed in orbit. SpaceX thus underlined the operational nature of the test. The vehicle served not merely as a technology demonstrator, but was integrated directly into the expansion of the company’s proprietary communications infrastructure.

The decisive technical crucible, however, came during re-entry and the approach to the Texan coast. Prior to the final manoeuvre, the firm stressed that a catch attempt would be sanctioned only after a thorough on-orbit systems check; any anomalies would have necessitated a controlled splashdown in the ocean. Yet the telemetry cleared the risky approach. The 52-metre projectile shed its velocity, hovered in the immediate vicinity of the launch tower, and was ultimately clasped and secured mid-flight with remarkable precision by two massive mechanical arms—dubbed 'chopsticks' in company parlance. The manoeuvre spares the vehicle heavy landing legs and gear, shifting the structural burden to the ground infrastructure and thereby conserving precious payload capacity.

The full reusability of an orbital launch system hinges not on the return of the first stage, but on mastering the thermal and mechanical rigours of the payload section's re-entry.

II. Light and shade of the fifteenth flight: A divided balance sheet

Despite the jubilation of the engineers and onlookers on site, a sober appraisal of the flight profile reveals that the fifteenth test was no unqualified triumph. The primary objective had been conceived as a historic double catch: both components of the system were to return undamaged to Starbase within minutes or hours, rather than being scuttled at sea or recovered as on earlier flights. This ambition foundered early on the first stage. Powered by its Raptor engines, the Super Heavy booster initiated its return burn as planned, but aborted the manoeuvre abruptly after roughly six and a half minutes.

An automated abort protocol within the guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) system precluded a return to the launch tower. On Saturday morning, SpaceX spokesman Dan Huot dispassionately explained the rationale behind the system's decision: sensors had detected that the booster was not in the exact position required to hazard a catch attempt. The software acted conservatively by design to avert catastrophic damage to the highly complex ground installation. Elon Musk, the chief executive, later added on X that the booster was also running slightly low on propellant. Consequently, the 70-metre stage diverted to the Gulf of Mexico, hovered briefly above the water's surface, and exploded.

This incident tempers the mission's triumph, though it does not negate the progress made. SpaceX had already demonstrated the capture of the Super Heavy booster on three preceding flights. That the ostensibly mastered first stage should falter, whilst the vastly more complex recovery of the orbital upper stage succeeded, underscores the inherent volatility of the system. Professor Malcolm Macdonald, president of the Royal Aeronautical Society, encapsulated the dilemma: missing the first stage was a setback, but the successful return of the upper stage marked a substantial stride forward. Both halves of the recovery challenge have now been proven, albeit not yet within a single flight. The next milestone will be to knit these partial successes together and prove that the procedure can be operated reliably, repeatedly, and economically.

III. The economics of frequency: From costly prototype to orbital infrastructure

The economic logic behind the trials at Boca Chica can be understood only against the backdrop of spaceflight’s historical cost structures. Since 2015, when SpaceX first mastered the vertical landing of a first stage with the Falcon 9, the market has undergone a structural transformation. The Falcon family now logs more than a hundred launches a year. Reusing the lower stage has drastically slashed the cost of delivery to low-Earth orbit, securing SpaceX a dominant position in the commercial launch market. Yet the Falcon 9 remains an incomplete solution: its upper stage burns up irretrievably in the atmosphere after every mission.

Starship is the logical evolution of this approach, designed to eliminate the remaining hardware costs entirely. During a recent earnings call, Elon Musk emphasised that the test cadence for Starship is to be accelerated dramatically: from weekly flights to as many as two a week. The vision resembles that of commercial aviation, wherein a launch vehicle touches down, refuels, and takes off again within the shortest possible turnaround window. Yet such a rapid tempo is conceivable only if the landing takes place directly at the launch infrastructure, rendering elaborate recovery vessels, cranes, and weeks of refurbishment at sea obsolete. Catching the upper stage following a ballistic re-entry is by far the more formidable undertaking than decelerating the comparatively sluggish first stage.

The economic urgency underpinning this effort has intensified of late. Following SpaceX’s conversion into a public company earlier this year, institutional shareholders are scrutinising the Starship programme's capital discipline and milestones with heightened vigilance. Every lost prototype and every delay in certifying full reusability weighs upon the company's valuation. While the capacity to haul 26 hefty Starlink V3 satellites into orbit on a single flight hints at the commercial upside, so long as both stages cannot be recovered simultaneously and flawlessly, the radical cost reductions promised by Mr Musk remain an actuarial projection.

IV. Geopolitical alignments and the contest for the Moon

Beyond SpaceX’s commercial ambitions, the Starship programme carries substantial geopolitical weight, intertwined as it is with NASA’s strategy. Under the Artemis programme, the United States aims to return astronauts to the lunar surface in 2028—a timeline widely regarded by specialists as exceptionally ambitious. SpaceX serves as a critical contractor: the firm is tasked with adapting the Starship upper stage into a specialised lunar lander to ferry astronauts from lunar orbit to the surface and back.

NASA’s leadership is observing progress with visible relief, but also with tacit anxiety over the pace of development to date. The NASA administrator, Jared Isaacman—a close ally of Mr Musk who was nominated by Donald Trump at the billionaire's behest—commented on X following the fifteenth test that 'converts are made every day', describing the milestone as another step towards the Moon. Nevertheless, it is impossible to ignore that the broader tempo of Starship's development has lagged well behind the company's original targets. Before a crewed lunar landing can be realised, formidable feats such as orbital refuelling and translunar injection have yet to be demonstrated.

NASA has therefore deliberately avoided relying on a single provider, having concurrently commissioned Jeff Bezos’s tech venture, Blue Origin, to develop an alternative lunar lander alongside its New Glenn rocket. Both systems are slated to play a key role in the Artemis III mission, currently scheduled for the summer of 2027. Under this plan, four astronauts are to travel aboard NASA’s Orion spacecraft and dock with landers from SpaceX and Blue Origin. Yet Blue Origin has suffered severe setbacks of its own: a catastrophic explosion earlier this year completely destroyed the company’s launch pad in Florida. The resumption of flights has since been pushed back to the spring of 2027, ramping up the pressure on SpaceX even further.

Lurking behind this domestic American rivalry is mounting geopolitical urgency. Mr Isaacman has repeatedly cautioned that the United States is locked in a direct race with the People’s Republic of China. Beijing is pursuing a tightly marshalled, state-directed space programme aimed at landing Chinese taikonauts on the lunar surface by 2030. Should SpaceX fail to industrialise the full, reproducible reusability of its super-heavy freighter in the months ahead, the American timetable in this transpacific contest risks unravelling. The successful catch of the upper stage at Boca Chica was an indispensable step; the true trial of a dependable transport system, however, still lies ahead for Starship.

Historical context

The Space Shuttle paradigm

For decades, the partial reusability of the American Space Shuttle was regarded as the technological benchmark, yet it remained economically inefficient owing to exorbitant maintenance costs and lengthy turnaround cycles. The concept relied on winged orbiters touching down on conventional runways after re-entry, necessitating massive heat shields and heavy wing structures. SpaceX breaks with this tradition by dispensing with wings altogether, neutralising kinetic energy through direct vertical deceleration.

Ballistic re-entry

Re-entry from Earth orbit presents launch vehicles with radically different physical challenges from the suborbital flight of a first stage. Whilst boosters reach only a fraction of orbital velocity and can reverse course swiftly, orbital upper stages must shed speeds exceeding 27,000 kilometres per hour. Mastering these extreme thermal and aerodynamic loads dictates whether the structural reuse of spacecraft is technically viable at all.
