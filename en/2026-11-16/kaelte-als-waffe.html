Weaponising the Cold: Russia’s Strategic Offensive Against Ukrainian Energy Infrastructure

Between the systematic attrition of the civilian population and asymmetric retaliation on the home front

Edition of November 16, 2026 · Fact-checked against 6 sources · Deutsche Fassung
In brief
  • Russia’s attacks on transformer substations and power plants are designed to render Ukrainian cities uninhabitable during the winter and break civilian morale.
  • Ukraine is countering this attrition with massive drone strikes against Russian oil refineries and logistical bottlenecks such as the Crimean Bridge.
  • This military escalation is flanked by maximalist diplomatic demands from Moscow aimed at eroding the European security architecture.

The war in Eastern Europe has long ceased to be a purely territorial contest along static trenches. With the onset of freezing winter months, the Russian military leadership’s determination to treat its neighbour’s civilian infrastructure as a primary operational target has become unmistakably clear. The systematic destruction of substations, thermal power plants, and transmission grids is no mere collateral consequence of errant missiles; it reflects a calculated design. The winter cold itself has been weaponised to render urban life intolerable and coerce Ukrainian society into capitulation. Yet what appears at first glance to be an asymmetric war of attrition against the vulnerable is increasingly transforming into a double-edged confrontation, in which both sides seek to strike at the painful vulnerabilities of the other’s energy infrastructure.

I. The Logic of Attrition: The Grid as a Front Line

Severing vital lifelines strikes at the very core of modern civilisation. When electricity, running water, and district heating fail, metropolises of millions are reduced to inhospitable terrain within days. Recent barrages illustrate the stark consequences of this doctrine. In the southern Ukrainian oblast of Odesa alone, targeted strikes on energy facilities severed electricity to more than 52,200 households in an instant, according to the utility provider DTEK. Such operations are far from isolated. Russian tactics combine drone and missile strikes into concentrated waves calibrated to overwhelm Ukraine’s depleted air defences.

In doing so, the Russian leadership employs a grim systematic logic that may be understood as an extension of the classic Doktrin Douhet. Bombarding the foundations of civilian existence is intended to sap public morale, trigger fresh refugee flows towards Western Europe, and subject the government in Kyiv to unbearable domestic political pressure. Yet the history of strategic bombing has repeatedly demonstrated that societies facing existential threats exhibit remarkable adaptability and resilience. Rather than prompting panicked surrender, deprivation frequently fosters a pragmatic determination to endure, sustained by decentralised backup generators and emergency repairs undertaken by utility workers in mortal peril.

Moscow’s dismissal of these raids as ancillary military measures rings hollow. In major urban centres such as Dnipro and Zaporizhzhia, the strikes regularly claim civilian lives and reduce densely populated neighbourhoods to rubble. During nocturnal onslaughts involving upwards of 150 drones, Ukrainian forces may successfully down the vast majority—intercepting 131 of 151 drones in one recent raid—yet the warheads that breach defences suffice to pulverise substations and damage tugboats and transshipment terminals in ports such as Odesa. Compounding the devastation is the pernicious tactic of 'double-tap' strikes, in which rescue workers and police officers searching for survivors are targeted by follow-up drones minutes after the initial blast.

II. Kyiv’s Response: The Geometry of the Counter-Offensive

Faced with this existential threat, Ukraine has not sued for mercy; instead, it has cultivated an asymmetric strike capability deep inside Russian territory. Constrained by Western allies’ reluctance to permit the unrestricted use of long-range precision missiles against strategic targets in the Russian heartland, Ukraine’s domestic defence industry has developed a formidable fleet of long-range drones. The objective of this counter-offensive is equally calculated: the systematic disruption of Russian oil refining and logistics hubs critical to the Kremlin’s war machine.

The scale of this Ukrainian campaign has recently reached unprecedented proportions. The Russian defence ministry reported more than 1,300 Ukrainian drones targeting sites across the federation within a single 24-hour period. In the Moscow region alone, hundreds of drones were logged, with the mayor, Sergei Sobyanin, claiming that 191 were intercepted in the immediate vicinity of the capital. Even more damaging to Moscow is the attrition of its refining capacity. Drone strikes in the Rostov region, including on a refinery in the town of Azov, and at Ilsky in the North Caucasus, have inflicted severe damage and casualties.

The destruction of supply lines is no sideshow; it lies at the very centre of a war of attrition where victory belongs to the side with greater industrial and societal stamina.

President Volodymyr Zelensky set out the strategic dividends of these strikes, asserting that Ukraine has now paralysed or disrupted more than 55 per cent of Russia’s domestic oil refining capacity. Mr Zelensky made plain that this represents a legitimate response to Russia’s refusal to de-escalate and its unrelenting strikes against the Ukrainian power grid. This dynamic marks the emergence of a de facto Gleichgewicht des Schreckens at the infrastructural level. Although Ukraine commands vastly inferior overall resources compared to its nuclear-armed adversary, the vulnerability of sprawling, poorly shielded industrial facilities—such as refineries and bridges—compels Moscow to redeploy precious air-defence assets away from the front line to safeguard its own interior.

At the same time, symbolically potent and militarily vital infrastructure remains fixed in Ukraine’s sights. A further drone strike against the arch of the Crimean Bridge across the Kerch Strait prompted the immediate suspension of vehicular traffic, leaving thousands of motorists stranded in hours-long queues. Even if Moscow’s installed governor, Sergei Aksyonov, declined to specify the extent of the damage, the repeated targeting of this artery demonstrates that Kyiv will not leave the logistics of the occupied peninsula unmolested.

III. Sham Negotiations and Geopolitical Blackmail

While strikes on energy infrastructure are calibrated to maximise physical hardship for civilians, the Kremlin is staging a diplomatic charade calculated primarily to disorient and divide the West. In exploratory trilateral talks held in Abu Dhabi between Russian, Ukrainian, and American representatives, Moscow tabled demands rightly dismissed by European diplomats as utterly divorced from reality. Rather than negotiating a viable ceasefire, Russia demanded the total withdrawal of Ukrainian forces from the Donbas, permanent neutrality for Kyiv, and the rollback of NATO infrastructure to its pre-1997 borders before eastern enlargement.

The European Union’s new foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas, delivered a trenchant assessment of these tactics ahead of a meeting of European foreign ministers in Brussels. Dismissing Moscow’s terms as 'absurd', she pointed to Russia’s familiar negotiating playbook: advancing maximalist positions and engineering synthetic escalations in order to present minor subsequent concessions as compromise. Ms Kallas urged European partners to show clarity and resolve, arguing that the EU must recognise its own leverage—not least sanctions and the more than €200bn in immobilised Russian central bank assets held at institutions such as Euroclear in Belgium.

Yet the diplomatic reality is beset by internal discord and institutional friction. Although Ms Kallas is herself excluded from the newly configured European negotiating team—comprising representatives of Poland, Germany, Italy, France, the United Kingdom, and the EU leadership—countries such as Austria are pressing for a 15-point plan based on incremental, localised truces. Other ministers, including the Swedish foreign minister, Maria Malmer Stenergard, harbour fundamental doubts regarding Russia’s willingness to negotiate, calling instead for a sharp increase in military pressure. The geopolitical terrain is further complicated by impending summits; many capitals are watching with unease as plans take shape for meetings between President Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin, and China’s Xi Jinping in Shenzhen.

IV. The Erosion of the European Security Order

Beneath Russia’s maximalist posture lies a more perilous objective that extends well beyond Ukraine. Moscow is purposefully seeking to fracture the unity of the Western defensive alliance. Following the talks in the United Arab Emirates, Russian negotiator Alexei Drobinin hinted that Moscow would entertain bilateral non-aggression pacts with individual European NATO members, accompanied by bespoke security guarantees. Such an arrangement would hollow out the core of Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty, eroding the very principle of collective defence.

Former Russian diplomats such as Boris Bondarev have issued stark warnings against the perils of such transactional fragmentation. Were European states to enter into bilateral pacts of this nature, NATO would collapse de facto, leaving individual European nations isolated and exposed before Moscow. It is a calculated bid to drive a wedge between Eastern European frontline states, the Baltic republics, the Nordic region, and the Western European interior. It is hardly surprising that Estonia, mindful of the latent menace, is already weighing the complete closure of its border crossings with the Russian Federation.

Simultaneously, Moscow is banking on time and political shifts within Western capitals. The calculus of attrition presumes that domestic political upheaval in democratic nations will gradually erode the appetite for aid. As prominent political factions in key states such as France or Germany demand an unwinding of sanctions and an end to arms shipments, the Kremlin expects to reap the dividends of its winter campaign. At the intersection of physical strikes on substations, threats to allied solidarity, and weaponised disinformation, the thermal assault on Ukrainian heating and the hybrid offensive against European order merge into a single, coherent strategy.

The European security order thus faces a trial of historic magnitude. Shielding Ukraine’s energy infrastructure with robust air defences and repelling Russian attempts at division are not distinct operational concerns; they are two sides of the same coin. Should the West falter in resisting Moscow’s coercion through cold and its nuclear-tinged maximalism, the coming winter will be dark and freezing not only for the citizens of Kyiv, Dnipro, and Odesa, but the very foundation of European security will suffer irreparable harm.

Historical context

The Douhet Doctrine and Strategic Air Warfare

The theory formulated by Giulio Douhet in the 1920s posited that massive aerial bombardment of the civilian hinterland would shatter a nation’s will to resist. Yet historical precedents, from the air raids of the Second World War to the Vietnam War, have consistently refuted the assumption of swift moral collapse. Instead, the destruction of vital infrastructure has tended to harden collective resolve and survival instincts.

Balance of Terror and Mutual Vulnerability

Developed during the Cold War, the concept of mutual vulnerability held that shared exposure to a devastating strike engendered stability through deterrence. In today’s conventional war of drones and attrition, this principle is transposed onto the industrial base. When both belligerents possess the capacity to dismantle an adversary’s vital supply lines, a novel and highly volatile escalatory dynamic emerges.
