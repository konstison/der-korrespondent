European banking in flux: Brussels gives its blessing

The approval of UniCredit’s takeover of Commerzbank marks a watershed for Europe's financial architecture, forcing Berlin’s hand.

AI-generated · Edition of November 16, 2026 · Fact-checked against 5 sources · Deutsche Fassung
In brief
  • The European Commission has unconditionally approved the acquisition of Frankfurt-based Commerzbank by Italian banking giant UniCredit.
  • Despite fierce political resistance in Berlin and from within the institution itself, UniCredit had previously built up a stake of nearly 50 per cent and secured the assent of the ECB.
  • The move marks a departure from national preservation of the status quo towards an activist European industrial policy determined to forge continental banking champions.

For years, the European banking landscape has lingered in a peculiar state of paralysis. Whilst political institutions in Brussels and Frankfurt ceaselessly championed the grand project of an integrated single market, retail and commercial lending remained resolutely fragmented along national borders. With the European Commission’s decision to clear the takeover of Frankfurt-based Commerzbank by Italy’s UniCredit, this era of masterly inactivity has come to an abrupt end. Brussels’ competition watchdogs found that the transaction raises no antitrust concerns. In doing so, the authority has swept aside a decisive hurdle to the first major cross-border bank acquisition since the global financial crisis. It is a regulatory signal whose significance extends far beyond the immediate fate of Germany’s second-largest private lender.

For the Federal Republic of Germany, the decision represents a painful watershed. For two years now, UniCredit has doggedly pursued the incorporation of Commerzbank into its fold. What began as a discreet, softly-softly approach rapidly escalated into an open contest of wills. In the teeth of fierce opposition from the federal government in Berlin and Commerzbank’s management board in Frankfurt, the Milanese bank built up an equity and derivative position of nearly 50 per cent. Neither political rebukes nor solemn warnings about the erosion of national economic sovereignty managed to halt the Italian advance. With the European Central Bank having already signalled its regulatory assent for the hostile bid, the European Union's executive arm has now thrown the door wide open. The message is unmistakable: Brussels is subordinating national sensitivities to the imperatives of pan-European banking consolidation.

I. The End of National Hegemony over Credit

The Commission’s decision draws a line under an era in which member-state governments could blithely rely on shielding their systemically important domestic champions from unwanted foreign suitors. Historically, Commerzbank has been viewed in Germany as an indispensable pillar of the country's manufacturing model—the quintessential Hausbank and primary financier to the vaunted Mittelstand of small and medium-sized enterprises. German mid-caps rely heavily on close, long-standing relationships with domestic lenders attuned to local manufacturing conditions, supply-chain vulnerabilities, and regional idiosyncrasies. Citing this vital relationship, critics in Berlin persistently insisted that the bank’s independence had to be safeguarded on broader macroeconomic grounds.

Such arguments carried no weight in Brussels. Teresa Ribera, the European Commissioner for Competition, made it plain that the merger would not impede effective competition; rather, it was deemed essential to bolstering the resilience of the European financial sector as a whole. Europe requires stronger, larger institutions capable of expanding, investing, and underwriting businesses and households alike, according to the Commission’s reasoning. Cross-border mergers, it argued, represent the most effective vehicle for unlocking economies of scale, paring back operating overheads, and insulating lenders against asymmetric macroeconomic shocks. The verdict is unvarnished: where Berlin discerns a peril to credit provision for domestic industry, Brussels sees merely the overdue unwinding of archaic structural inefficiencies.

That UniCredit was able to assemble a stake approaching half of Commerzbank’s equity despite vocal protests from Berlin exposes the stark limits of national defensive barriers under EU law. Provided an acquirer commands sufficient regulatory capital, complies with the prudential criteria of the ECB, and does not create a dominant position in the antitrust sense, the European treaties offer no legal lever to scupper a takeover on purely patriotic grounds. The treaty-enshrined freedoms of establishment and the free movement of capital consistently trump the political discomfort of sovereign member states.

II. The Battle for Global Competitiveness

Beneath the European Commission’s regulatory posture lies a profound shift in the economic philosophy of the continent’s governing elites. For over a decade, economists and central bankers have watched with mounting anxiety as European lenders fell ever further behind their global peers. Whilst Wall Street’s behemoths swiftly cleaned up their balance sheets after 2008, captured international market share in investment banking, and achieved market valuations many multiples of their European counterparts, Europe's lenders remained trapped in a morass of sub-par profitability and parochial home markets. This lack of financial heft has ultimately choked off the supply of risk capital and enfeebled the continent’s innovative capacity.

Accordingly, the European Union has resolved this year to undertake a sweeping overhaul of its merger guidelines. The overarching objective of these reforms is nothing less than the deliberate cultivation of 'European champions'. Brussels is progressively abandoning an exclusively static, intra-market definition of competition that historically strangled cross-border tie-ups on the basis of narrow regional market shares. In its place, a consensus has emerged that European corporates can only compete on equal terms with American or Asian titans if they are permitted to achieve commensurate scale on their home continent.

UniCredit’s acquisition of Commerzbank serves as the ultimate litmus test for this emerging doctrine. UniCredit has explicitly stated its ambition to cement its position as a true European heavyweight. A group with dominant franchises in both Italy and Germany can unlock efficiencies in information technology, risk modelling, and debt issuance on international capital markets that would remain hopelessly out of reach for two balance sheets operating in isolation. In the eyes of the Brussels competition authorities, the approval demonstrates that it is entirely possible to foster formidable continental champions without sacrificing fair competition or consumer choice at the national level.

III. An Incomplete Architecture and Its Inherent Contradictions

Yet for all the regulatory clarity, the transaction lays bare fundamental paradoxes within the project of European integration itself. For more than ten years, member states have wrestled over the completion of the banking union. While single supervision under the ECB and harmonised resolution mechanisms were duly established, the third and vital pillar—a common European deposit insurance scheme (EDIS)—has foundered repeatedly on the veto of Germany and other northern member states. The persistent fear of being held liable for legacy non-performing loans in southern European banking systems has stymied meaningful reform.

Herein lies an exquisite irony: for years, German politicians and bank executives asserted that genuine cross-border integration could only occur once balance-sheet risks were cleansed and fiscal rectitude was entrenched across the periphery. Today, this integration is being delivered not through tortuous diplomatic communiqués and EU directives, but via the capital markets. An Italian lender that assiduously executed its operational turnaround and repaired its balance sheet is taking advantage of European freedoms to acquire a venerable German institution. The market is establishing hard facts on the ground while intergovernmental diplomacy remains stuck in an impasse.

The clearance of the Commerzbank takeover demonstrates that the genuine market integration of Europe's financial sector can no longer be held hostage to the absence of political consensus.

At the same time, institutional friction is inevitable. So long as the banking architecture remains half-built, cross-border banking groups will always face unresolved questions over the fungibility of liquidity and capital between parent and subsidiary entities in a crisis. When systemic stress hits, national regulators instinctively resort to ring-fencing capital within domestic borders to protect local depositors. Berlin’s scepticism is therefore born not merely of bruised national pride, but of the legitimate operational dread that managerial control and risk-bearing capacity might be siphoned off to Milan in times of distress. Brussels' ruling forces European policymakers to confront these unaddressed design flaws in the banking union without illusions.

IV. A New Doctrine for Europe's Single Market

The decision also signals a historic course correction in the treatment of corporate megamergers. For years, European antitrust policy laboured under the reputation of being excessively dogmatic, detecting anti-competitive dominance even where enterprises were simply striving for the scale required to survive globally. The memory of Alstom and Siemens remains raw: their proposed rail merger was vetoed by the then-competition commissioner, a ruling that drew blistering condemnation from Berlin and Paris alike.

Today, Brussels is falling into line with the new geo-economic paradigm. The current Commission is keen to underscore that industrial scale is not a vice, but an indispensable prerequisite for systemic resilience. If European commercial banks lack the balance-sheet depth to underwrite the colossal capital expenditures required for the green transition and technological modernisation, that lucrative advisory and financing business will default to Wall Street. Subordinating policy to the diktat of global competitiveness will require member states to accept the disappearance of cherished national flagships.

For Commerzbank and its workforce, a period of sweeping restructuring now begins. Absorption into the UniCredit empire will inevitably entail rationalisation, the integration of legacy IT platforms, and a strategic overhaul of the corporate lending division. For German economic policy, meanwhile, the verdict from Brussels is an unvarnished reality check: in an integrated single market, strategic industrial sway cannot be preserved by retreat into defensive domestic protectionism, but solely through competitive enterprise operating across the European stage. Brussels has laid down the tracks; Europe’s financial markets will henceforth run on entirely new rules.

Historical context

The European banking union

In the wake of the 2008 financial crash and the sovereign debt crisis, EU member states agreed to establish a banking union to mutualise risk and centralise supervision. Its core pillars comprise the Single Supervisory Mechanism under the auspices of the ECB and the Single Resolution Mechanism. However, its completion—most notably a common European deposit insurance scheme—has languished for years amid political discord between core and peripheral member states.

The collapse of the Alstom-Siemens rail tie-up

In 2019, the European Commission prohibited the planned merger of the rail transport divisions of Siemens and Alstom, citing concerns over excessive market concentration and price increases in European rolling stock. The decision caused considerable dismay in Berlin and Paris, prompting loud demands for a reform of European competition law. To this day, it stands as the textbook example of the conflict between stringent antitrust enforcement and the political drive to build globally competitive industrial champions.
