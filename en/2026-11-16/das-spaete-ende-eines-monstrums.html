The Belated Fall of a Monster: Gérard Depardieu and France’s Break with the Culture of Impunity

By abandoning his appeal, French cinema’s most formidable figure has capitulated before the law, sealing the demise of an entire era.

Edition of November 16, 2026 · Fact-checked against 4 sources · Deutsche Fassung
In brief
  • By withdrawing his appeal, Gérard Depardieu accepts a definitive 18-month suspended sentence for sexual assault and must now register as a sex offender.
  • The 77-year-old actor, who embodied over half a century of French cinematic history across more than 200 productions, admitted his misconduct, abandoning his long-standing shield of bawdy humour.
  • With this verdict and an impending rape trial, France’s cultural establishment is undertaking a painful yet long overdue reckoning with its authoritarian myths and ingrained codes of silence.

There are legal proceedings whose significance extends far beyond the courtroom register. When Gérard Depardieu’s counsel announced on a Monday morning, at the opening of his appeal hearing, that the 77-year-old actor was withdrawing his challenge against a prior conviction for sexual assault, more was concluded than a mere courtroom dispute. At that moment, a figurehead of European cinema capitulated before a legal order that had scarcely seemed to touch him for more than half a century. The judgment—imposing an 18-month suspended sentence and ordering his inclusion on the sex offenders register—is now final. The public prosecution and the civil parties formally joined the withdrawal. What remains is neither a heroic exit nor a tragic paradox, but the unvarnished finding of criminal guilt. Depardieu, who across more than 200 films and television productions projected the countenance, physique, and pathos of the French Republic onto the screen like no other, is now before the law precisely what the verdict declares: a convicted sex offender.

I. The Silence Breaks Before the Bench

The acts adjudicated by the Paris court and now definitively penalised did not occur in the hazy twilight of distant decades, but in 2021, on the set of Jean Becker’s feature film Les Volets Verts. The complainants—a set dresser and an assistant director—alleged assaults that the defence long countered with the standard playbook familiar from such trials. Depardieu had initially denied the allegations. Yet the weight of evidence, bolstered by compromising footage documenting the atmosphere on set, proved overwhelming. The statement issued by the actor via Agence France-Presse on the day the appeal was due to commence marked a striking rupture. «Justice has spoken. I have been found guilty. I hear it and I accept this conviction», he announced. It was a capitulation to a reality that refused to yield to the actor’s customary theatricality.

Remarkable about this statement, however, was not merely the formal surrender, but the attempt at retrospective self-exposure, which shook the traditional lexicon of the milieu. Depardieu conceded that he had long hidden behind «provocation and coarse humour» on film sets. He acknowledged that his remarks and «some of my gestures» were not «without consequence», offering his «profound and sincere apologies» to those who may have suffered as a result. The pivotal sentence followed immediately: he had come to understand, Depardieu said, that intent does not alter the harm inflicted. This admission strikes at the foundation of a defence strategy deployed for years, which routinely reinterpreted overstepping boundaries as harmless banter, exuberant high spirits, or mere misunderstanding. By renouncing the primacy of intent and acknowledging the reality of the harm caused, the actor dismantled his own narrative: that of an untamed colossus who was merely too boisterous.

II. Dismantling the «Monstre sacré»

To measure the scale of this implosion, one must recall the mythological status Depardieu enjoyed in France. He was not merely a distinguished exponent of auteur cinema; he was a national monument. From the 1970s onwards, he was seen as the personification of a nation’s vitality, which saw reflected in his carnal heft, his tenderness, and his unrestrained directness a quintessential French trait. He was Cyrano de Bergerac, Danton, Jean Valjean, and Obelix rolled into one. This fusion of actor and national canon gave rise to the archetype of the Monstre sacré—that untouchable, transcendent figure whose creative brilliance was treated as a licence for unrestrained excess. What would have been deemed a grave offence in an ordinary mortal was transfigured in him into a creative force of nature, proof of an unbridled spirit refusing to submit to bourgeois standards.

This veneration masked a perilous conflation of aesthetic liberty with personal impunity. For decades, French audiences and critics alike applauded the phenomenon of Depardieu, greeting accounts of his outrageous conduct on set with a knowing smile. Excess was treated as an integral component of the total work of art. Commentators celebrated his unpredictability, were amused by his television provocations, and studiously ignored the voices of those exposed to his physical and verbal predations. In this arrangement, cinema functioned as an extraterritorial sanctuary. Where art was being created, ran the tacit assumption, bourgeois constraints had no place. Yet this construct served not to protect art, but to entrench patriarchal prerogative. In retrospect, the Depardieu affair illustrates the ruthless consistency with which the concept of genius was instrumentalised to conceal a sordid reality of coercion behind an aura of the sublime.

The disenchantment of genius does not arise from the loss of its art, but from the return of the rule of law to the film set.

With the definitive ruling in Paris, this protective cloak has finally disintegrated. A man registered as a sex offender can no longer serve as a vessel of national identification. The myth of the untamed natural force has been stripped down to its legal reality: the conduct of a man who shamelessly exploited his position of power over female crew members. The «provocation» Depardieu lamented was, in truth, nothing more than the display of an asymmetric power dynamic, in which extras, assistants, and technicians were left defenceless against the star’s whims.

III. The Implosion of the Culture of Complicity

Depardieu’s personal fall is, however, inextricably bound up with the institutional failure of French cinema. For years, the industry operated under the unwritten rules of a deeply rooted Kultur der Duldung. A complex web of commercial interdependence, professional solidarity, and hierarchical deference served to hush up transgressions. Producers feared for their investments, directors for the goodwill of their leading man, and junior crew members for their careers should they dare cross the most powerful figure in the business. French cinema, which prided itself on being the vanguard of free expression and a bastion of intellectual independence, revealed itself in practice to be a remarkably feudal ecosystem ruled by the law of the mighty.

Compared with its European neighbours, France long struggled to absorb the shocks of the MeToo movement. Whilst careers were terminated and industry practices overhauled across the Anglophone world in short order, resistance initially coalesced along the Seine. Critics scented a puritanical threat to the French art of seduction, defended artistic freedom, and occasionally conflated the liberty to desire with the right to harass. During this rearguard action, Depardieu was briefly elevated into an emblem of an endangered libertarian tradition. Only a few years ago, prominent voices mobilised to warn against a public lynching, defending the actor’s entitlement to pursue his craft undisturbed. Reality has now caught up with these rationalisations in brutal fashion.

That this culture of complicity ultimately collapsed was not due to any sudden moral epiphany within the industry, but to the persistence of the victims and the emergence of irrefutable evidence. The unearthing of footage, which the NZZ astutely identified as the turning point, rendered decades of looking away untenable. The mechanisms of downplaying evaporated once the reality of his behaviour was laid bare in picture and sound. The complaint brought by the two women on the set of Les Volets Verts formed the breach in the dam. It proved that the silence could be broken, even when the accused was the country’s most celebrated figure. Through this trial, French society has absorbed the painful lesson that art is not an extraterritorial zone, and that a film set must answer to the same standards of employment and criminal law as any ordinary workplace.

IV. The End of Impunity as an Epochal Shift

Depardieu’s acceptance of the verdict by no means marks the end of his legal tribulations. On the contrary, it is merely the prologue to a far graver proceeding. In 2025, a French investigating magistrate ordered Depardieu to stand trial in a separate case on charges of raping the actress Charlotte Arnould in 2018. This impending trial will once again compel the industry to confront an era in which complaints were dismissed and victims sidelined. The judicial confirmation of his guilt in the Becker case deprives the actor of any ability to present himself in the upcoming trial as the blameless victim of a media campaign. The credibility of the myth has already been shattered.

The belated demise of this «monster» therefore represents an epochal watershed for French culture. It marks the conclusion of an era in which creative brilliance served as a blanket moral absolution. Society is casting aside the convenient illusion that artists must stand outside the civil law to produce enduring work. What Depardieu acknowledged in his statement—that subjective intent cannot nullify objective harm—is the crux of this paradigm shift. It is no longer a question of whether a perpetrator regarded himself as charming, forceful, or merely eccentric; what matters exclusively is the physical and psychological integrity of those subject to his power.

In Gérard Depardieu, French cinema loses one of its most formidable screens of projection. His films will endure, but the gaze cast upon them has been irrevocably altered. It will no longer be possible to watch Cyrano de Bergerac without perceiving the grimace of an overweening abuse of power that this verdict has definitively recorded. Yet this loss of innocence is no injury to culture; it is the price of its maturity. The end of impunity does not herald the end of creative freedom; rather, it emancipates art from being held hostage by an unbridled ego. With the monument fallen, the path is clear for a fairer, soberer reality in which talent no longer excuses the crime.

Historical context

The Myth of the «Monstre sacré»

The concept of the 'sacred monster' gained currency in France during the late 19th and early 20th centuries to describe larger-than-life stage figures such as Sarah Bernhardt. It conveyed the Romantic premise that extraordinary creative vitality and eccentricity stood above bourgeois conventions. In post-war cinema, the term hardened into a convenient alibi, trivialising overt abuse in the name of artistic genius.

The French Culture of Complicity

For generations, French auteur cinema was underpinned by a tacit pact between directors, producers, and performers that dismissed off-camera transgressions as creative furore. Sustained by hierarchical dependence, this silence shielded the abuse of power on film sets from legal accountability for decades. The delayed yet potent arrival of the MeToo movement in Paris finally exposed these institutionalised sanctuaries.
