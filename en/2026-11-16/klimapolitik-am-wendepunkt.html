Climate Policy at a Turning Point: The illusion of resilience

As COP31 convenes in Antalya, waning carbon sinks and missed emissions targets expose the limits of multilateral crisis management.

Edition of November 16, 2026 · Fact-checked against 6 sources · Deutsche Fassung
In brief
  • As the 31st UN Climate Change Conference opens in Antalya, a multi-year breach of the 1.5°C limit appears inevitable, whilst UN estimates warn that global policies are steering the planet towards 2.8°C of warming.
  • Although fossil-fuel CO2 emissions fell marginally to just over 38 gigatonnes, this dip stems primarily from the wartime energy shock triggered by the conflict involving Iran rather than any systemic structural transition.
  • At the same time, terrestrial and marine carbon sinks are weakening markedly: protracted droughts and wildfires fuelled by a powerful El Niño event have already stripped land-based sinks of around a fifth of their buffering capacity.

As tens of thousands of diplomats, negotiators and observers gather on Turkey’s Mediterranean coast this week for the 31st UN Climate Change Conference to debate the fate of the global thermostat, the setting is deceitfully serene. For decades, the diplomatic ritual of climate summits has adhered to a well-rehearsed choreography: alarming findings from Earth system science, tortuous drafting sessions in windowless plenary halls, and solemnly delivered compromise texts hammered out in the bleary dawn of inevitable overtime. Yet the opening of COP31 in Antalya is overshadowed by a development that fundamentally destabilises the very foundations of climate diplomacy. A decade after the entry into force of the Paris Agreement, the fiction that climate change can be smoothly managed has run aground on the unyielding shoals of physics. The scientific assessments underpinning the conference leave no room for complacency. The 1.5°C target—once established as a normative guardrail to avert existential perils for vulnerable island states and low-lying coastal regions—is giving way, with mathematical dispassion, to a far harsher reality.

I. The Deceptive Respite in Emissions Data

At first glance, the 21st Global Carbon Budget report, compiled with contributions from institutions such as the Alfred Wegener Institute for Polar and Marine Research and Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich, offers an ostensibly comforting figure. Global carbon dioxide emissions from the combustion of coal, oil and gas edged down slightly last year, totalling just over 38 gigatonnes of CO2. Following years of uninterrupted growth—fossil emissions rose by an annual average of nearly 1% between 2016 and 2025, and by just over 1% in 2025 alone—this figure marks a statistical break. Yet anyone inferring the dawn of a structural, permanent turning point misreads the economic and geopolitical drivers at play.

The dip in fossil emissions is only partly attributable to the accelerating green transition. To be sure, the solar market continues its exponential expansion, whilst rising electric vehicle adoption has visibly tempered fossil fuel demand growth. Environmental scientists calculate that the deployment of solar, wind and electric mobility averted nearly two-thirds of the additional 0.8bn tonnes of CO2 that would otherwise have been generated by rising global energy demand. Nevertheless, these impressive gains cannot single-handedly offset the expanding appetite for primary energy. The underside of the coin becomes apparent in the granular consumption figures: whilst oil demand contracted by nearly 2% and natural gas experienced a modest dip, emissions from burning coal—the dirtiest of all fossil fuels—rose by a further 0.6%. The primary engine of this growth remains the swelling energy hunger of emerging economies such as India, which, irrespective of international appeals for restraint, are underpinning their industrial expansion with dependable, domestic baseload power.

That aggregate global emissions did not climb was therefore not the outcome of collective ecological enlightenment, but rather of an exogenous shock to global trade networks. Armed conflict involving Iran and the consequent closure of the strategically vital Strait of Hormuz precipitated a severe energy crunch. A drastically constrained and costlier supply of crude oil and liquefied natural gas forced industry and households across multiple regions into involuntary rationing, dampening economic output. Such crisis-driven contractions possess no structural permanence. Once geopolitical risk premia subside or alternative shipping routes are secured, emissions threaten to revert swiftly to their previous trajectory alongside economic recovery. The hope for a peaceful, orderly decarbonisation driven by intrinsic momentum finds no support in the real-world data of 2026.

Structural stagnation of emissions at a 38-gigatonne plateau is no testament to geo-economic transformation; it is the bill for wartime supply crunches and misallocated capital.

II. Breaching the Paris Guardrail

The deceptive calm of the emissions data contrasts starkly with the state of the atmosphere. Observations from the European Earth observation service Copernicus depict a planet whose thermal shock-absorbers are fast running out. The year 2026 is almost certain to become the second year on record, after 2024, in which global average temperatures breach 1.5°C above pre-industrial baselines. Meteorologists reckon it will very likely rank as the second-warmest year since systematic record-keeping began, with a 20% probability of eclipsing the historic high of 2024.

From a scientific standpoint, surpassing the threshold in an isolated year does not constitute a formal failure of the Paris Agreement, which rests on long-term climatic averages. Yet this methodological caveat is shedding both political and practical relevance. Copernicus forecasts indicate that the four-year average spanning 2023 to 2026 will now also settle above the 1.5°C line. What was once suspicion is hardening into certainty: humanity is no longer standing before a hypothetical threshold, but is already navigating a phase of prolonged transgression, euphemistically termed an 'overshoot' in diplomatic parlance.

Recent meteorological extremes underscore the severity of this shift. This past October proved to be the warmest October ever recorded worldwide, with a global mean surface temperature of 15.35°C—a departure of 1.75°C from the pre-industrial benchmark. It marked the third consecutive month to hold above the 1.5°C threshold. In Europe, which logged its second-hottest October in history, eastern and north-eastern regions suffered under exceptional heat. The hydrological fallout was immediate: critical waterways such as the Rhine, the Danube and the Vistula shrank to precariously low levels, whilst elsewhere catastrophic flash floods carved swathes of destruction from Ireland and Britain to Spain, Greece and Turkey.

This trend is being magnified by an oceanic anomaly. An exceptionally intense El Niño in the equatorial Pacific is driving temperatures still higher. Between 60° north and 60° south, sea surface temperatures reached a historic peak of 20.95°C in October, eclipsing the previous record set in 2023. In the central and eastern Pacific, temperature anomalies surged to as much as 3.21°C above the long-term average—a departure that outstrips even the intense episodes of 1997-98 and 2015-16. The fallout for marine ecosystems is dire: ocean warming is pushing coral bleaching to unprecedented heights worldwide, destroying the foundation of countless marine species. The oceans are absorbing colossal amounts of heat that they will release only sluggishly, locking the climate system's thermal memory into continued warming for years to come.

III. The Faltering of Natural Buffers

Yet the most alarming development confronting delegates in Antalya lies beyond headline emissions tallies and thermometer readings. It concerns the Earth system's underlying biogeochemical cycle: the creeping breakdown of natural carbon sinks. Historically, the oceans and the terrestrial biosphere have served as humanity’s indispensable shield. Roughly half of all anthropogenic CO2 emissions were reliably sequestered by forests, soils, peatlands and the seas, keeping them from accumulating in the atmosphere.

This mechanism is beginning to buckle under the weight of sustained warming. As the geographer Julia Pongratz observed in recent analyses, land and oceans could, under optimal conditions, absorb considerably more carbon; yet human-induced climate change is acting as an increasingly hostile countervailing force. On land, persistent droughts, heatwaves and altered precipitation regimes have already erased roughly a fifth of the terrestrial sink's potential buffering capacity. While afforestation and reforestation schemes across the globe cushion some of these losses, planted saplings cannot readily compensate for the widespread degradation of mature, functioning ecosystems.

Nowhere is this dynamic more pronounced than in tropical rainforests and wetlands. For decades, global emissions from land-use change had trended downwards, aided by stricter conservation policies—not least across the Brazilian Amazon. Over the past year, however, this progress went into reverse. The catalyst was a wave of catastrophic fires in South East Asia, where a severe El Niño coincided with the widespread combustion of drained peatlands and cleared forests. Peat fires are insidiously destructive: smouldering deep underground, they are almost impossible to extinguish and release immense stores of carbon built up over millennia within a matter of weeks. The Earth system is flashing signs of a pernicious feedback loop: climate-induced weather extremes destroy the very vegetation indispensable for sequestering greenhouse gases. If, as historical patterns suggest, the drought belt migrates from Asia to South America over the coming year, the Amazon basin faces a comparable inferno—with catastrophic implications for the global carbon balance.

IV. Antalya and the Inertia of Multilateralism

Against this backdrop, the agenda for COP31 in Antalya appears dispiritingly meek. Chaired by Turkey’s environment minister, Murat Kurum, delegates from nearly 200 nations are meeting within a format seeking fresh thematic framing, yet paralysed by familiar grievances. UN Secretary-General António Guterres struck a sharp tone ahead of the summit, warning that amid mounting global heat deaths, droughts and deluge—such as the recent disasters in China and Nepal—the world could ill afford to remain bogged down in endless procedural wrangling. Yet climate diplomacy remains hobbled by fundamental contradictions.

Turkey, as host, has championed a headline proposal for a global electrification target: increasing the share of electricity in final energy consumption from 20% today to 35% by 2035. Countries including Germany, whose delegation is led by environment minister Carsten Schneider, have thrown their weight behind the plan. The strategic rationale is obvious: electrifying transport, heating and industrial processes should throttle direct fossil-fuel combustion. Yet the initiative harbours an inherent paradox. Accelerated electrification benefits the climate only if the additional power is generated from zero-emission sources. Yet host nation Turkey continues to rely heavily on domestic coal power to fuel its own economic expansion.

The chasm between ambition and reality runs through the entire architecture of international climate governance. Whilst the latest UN projections indicate that currently implemented policies place the globe on track for a ruinous 2.8°C of warming by century’s end, the negotiating mandate remains trapped in haggling over diplomatic subtleties. The original Paris aspiration of capping warming at 1.5°C is scientifically untenable. What remains is an exercise in damage limitation: ensuring that any overshoot of this boundary is as brief and shallow as possible. Every fraction of a degree averted reduces the risk of triggering irreversible tipping points in the Earth system.

COP31 will not be remembered as a triumph over global warming, but rather as the moment when diplomacy was forced to acknowledge the obsolescence of its accounting models. When natural sinks fail and armed conflicts prove more effective at curbing emissions than international treaties, the multilateral toolkit demands an unsparing audit. The question in Antalya is no longer whether the 1.5°C limit will fall, but how humanity will navigate the era that follows.

Historical context

The 2015 Paris Agreement

At the COP21 UN climate summit in Paris, nearly two hundred nations entered into a legally binding commitment under international law to limit global warming to well below two degrees, and preferably to 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels. The treaty established a framework of nationally determined contributions based on ratcheting up ambition over time, yet it largely eschewed enforcement mechanisms for non-compliance. A decade on from its entry into force, a yawning gulf has opened between national pledges and actual emissions trajectories.

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation refers to a recurring, coupled oceanic and atmospheric circulation anomaly across the tropical Pacific. During El Niño episodes, prevailing trade winds slacken, triggering substantial sea-surface warming in the central and eastern Pacific and driving global weather extremes including droughts and torrential rainfall. The collision of such natural cycles with the anthropogenic baseline of global warming intensifies biogeochemical feedback loops and impairs the biosphere’s absorptive capacity.
