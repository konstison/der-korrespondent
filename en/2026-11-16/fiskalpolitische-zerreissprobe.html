A Fiscal Trial of Strength: The Union’s Internal Feud over Electricity Duty and Tax Relief

Caught between budgetary discipline and partisan posturing, the grand coalition reaches its fiscal limits

Edition of November 16, 2026 · Fact-checked against 5 sources · Deutsche Fassung
In brief
  • Whilst Federal Chancellor Friedrich Merz seeks to defer an across-the-board cut in electricity duty to 2027, senior CDU and CSU figures are pressing for the immediate implementation of their original election pledge.
  • CSU leader Markus Söder strictly refuses to tap the multi-billion-euro expansion of the mothers' pension for counter-financing, pointing instead to reserves in the Climate and Transformation Fund and windfall levies.
  • The looming loss of credibility, alongside the reservations of Federal Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil, turns the coalition committee into the arena for a fateful decision on the government's fiscal integrity.

The parliamentary summer recess has long since drawn to a close, and the political apparatus of the federal capital is gripped by a dispute that extends far beyond routine haggling over budget lines and fiscal carry-overs. In the negotiations between the CDU/CSU and the Social Democrats, a fundamental conflict is emerging that touches the very self-image of Germany's centre-right governing elites. At the heart of the matter lies a fiscal shortfall of some €5.4bn—the sum required to substantially reduce electricity duty not only for industrial manufacturing and the agricultural sector, but comprehensively for households and the Mittelstand as well. That this commitment, once framed as the Union's flagship pledge of relief for 2025, is absent from the recently agreed draft budget and was summarily deferred by Chancellor Friedrich Merz to 2027 has unleashed formidable centrifugal forces within the sister parties. At issue is nothing less than whether a government that took office on a platform of economic reason can honour its core fiscal pledges without reverting to old habits of expenditure expansion.

I. The Broken Promise and the Spectre of Credibility

The origins of the present conflict date back to the months of coalition formation and negotiations over the guiding principles of economic policy. The reduction in the tax burden on electrical energy was originally intended to benefit society as a whole. Strategists in the Union's parliamentary group regarded it as the clearest possible signal to an electorate unnerved by inflation and the costs of the green transition: a tangible step to bolster private purchasing power whilst curbing the cost of living. Yet fiscal reality swiftly caught up with the Chancellery. During the drafting of the federal budget, it became apparent that fiscal headroom was tightly circumscribed. The federal government resolved to exclude private households from the relief package for the time being, restricting the concessions to industry and agriculture. The Chancellor deferred citizens' expectations until 2027—a manoeuvre that provoked sharp rebuke not only from the opposition, but sent shockwaves through his own camp.

For the party base and senior Union representatives, this retreat carries substantial weight. The postponement strikes at the nerve centre of political credibility. When a core commitment to voters is revoked or kicked into the long grass during the very first phase of governing, the charge of electoral deceit risks sticking. In background briefings amongst parliamentarians, the depth of unease over this course is palpable. Fear is mounting that a government that wavers over the living costs of the broader population will permanently forfeit the trust of the bourgeois electorate. At the same time, an impression is taking hold that the Chancellery conceded without a fight to Social Democratic resistance against comprehensive tax cuts, simply to avoid imperilling a painstakingly negotiated budgetary peace.

The rancour is compounded by the fact that the problem of 'bracket creep'—the creeping tax increases driven by fiscal drag—remains unresolved. The combination of unadjusted income-tax brackets and an unabated electricity tax burden exerts tangible pressure on real incomes. Prominent voices within the Union argue that such a policy runs diametrically counter to the party's own manifesto. The Chancellor faces accusations of brandishing fiscal toughness primarily when it comes to offering relief to citizens, whilst substantive increases in expenditure elsewhere have been tolerated with barely a murmur.

The political tragedy of centre-right statecraft reveals itself at the precise moment when the safeguarding of partisan clientelist interests triumphs over the redemption of a general pledge of relief.

II. The Bavarian Veto and the Dogma of the Mothers' Pension

Amidst this already fragile state of affairs, vigorous resistance is mounting in Munich. CSU leader Markus Söder has made it abundantly clear that he has no intention of tolerating a postponement of the electricity tax cut. Speaking on ZDF's »Berlin direkt«, the Bavarian minister-president insisted that an agreement within the coalition remained within reach. More than that, Mr Söder elevated the reversal of the current course to a fundamental test of political integrity. At stake, he argued, was the standing of the entire governing coalition, which risked discrediting itself in the eyes of the public if it chose to ignore the plight of private households. This stance is bolstered by cabinet ministers from the sister party; Federal Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt publicly warned that relief on energy costs was urgently required and brooked no further delay.

Yet Mr Söder's solicitude for electricity consumers ends precisely where Bavarian trophy policies begin. When the CDU budget spokesman Mathias Middelberg publicly floated the prospect of funding the requisite billions by reallocating resources earmarked for the expansion of the mothers' pension, the CSU leadership responded with unyielding ferocity. Mr Middelberg had cautiously noted that all budgetary matters could, in principle, be discussed by the upcoming committee. For Mr Söder, however, any raid on the pension coffers is strictly out of the question. His rationale is disarmingly blunt: one would be ill-advised to close a credibility gap on electricity duty only to open another by repealing a statute already enacted into law. Acting in such a manner would be downright farcical.

Beneath this ostentatious steadfastness lies a naked assertion of partisan power. The expansion of the mothers' pension was wrung from coalition negotiations with the CDU and SPD by the CSU, and enshrined in law by the Bundestag in December 2025. It stands as the unmistakable trophy of Bavarian leverage in the federal arena. That this entitlement is to be funded entirely from general taxation from 2027 onwards, burdening the federal budget to the tune of around €5bn annually, is treated by Munich strategists as non-negotiable. For Mr Söder, the mothers' pension is a red line. Any retreat on the project would amount to political capitulation, inflicting severe damage on the CSU's standing with its rural and elderly core constituency. Thus, two competing expenditure claims confront each other within the Union, each defended under the banner of reliability—resulting in mutual fiscal gridlock.

III. The Hunt for Phantom Billions and Fiscal Orthodoxy

To deflect the charge of fiscal obstructionism, Markus Söder has tabled counter-proposals to finance the missing €5.4bn. Yet these proposals rely on fiscal instruments that have caused equal consternation in the Chancellery and the Federal Ministry of Finance. Mr Söder contended that, according to seasoned budget specialists, between three and four billion euros in unallocated funds could be mobilised from the Climate and Transformation Fund alone. Furthermore, the Bavarian minister-president floated a windfall tax on oil majors—a proposal that appears an alien fiscal body in a centre-right-led coalition and plays unmistakably to populist resentments.

The reactions to these overtures lay bare the profound fault line running through the coalition's architecture. Federal Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil, whose Social Democratic ministry demands adherence to fiscal discipline, insists unyieldingly on sound compensatory financing for any reduction in electricity duty. Simultaneously, the Federal Audit Office has weighed in, warning that winding down existing, economically inefficient subsidies represents the only viable route to creating fiscal space for lasting tax cuts, rather than inventing novel revenue streams such as special levies or diluting statutory ring-fencing.

The notion of summarily repurposing unspent resources from a special fund to subsidise general excise duties raises grave budgetary concerns. The Climate and Transformation Fund is designed, by definition, for long-term transformative investment. To abuse it as a fiscal reserve pot to paper over quotidian political disputes carries not only constitutional risks, but flouts the principles of transparent public accounting. Even more problematic is the proposed windfall tax: in public finance theory and practice, such an instrument is regarded as administratively unworkable, economically counterproductive, and legally highly vulnerable. Mr Söder's manoeuvre thus highlights the classic dilemma of a regional party that flies the flag of fiscal rectitude in public, yet resorts to elaborate sleights of hand when pressed for concrete funding sources, so long as its own sacred cows remain untouched.

IV. The Trial of Strength in the Coalition Committee

The adjudication of this ideological dispute over fiscal policy has now been referred to that body which, throughout the history of the Federal Republic, has invariably been tasked with unravelling insoluble contradictions: the coalition committee. When the leadership of the Union and the SPD convene at the Chancellery this Tuesday, far more will be at stake than technical horse-trading over statutory clauses and budget columns. What hangs in the balance is the strategic orientation of the grand coalition and the authority of the Chancellor. Friedrich Merz finds himself trapped in a pincer movement: should he insist on delaying the electricity tax cut until 2027, he will tarnish his reputation as a free-market reformer and invite the enduring wrath of substantial swathes of his own parliamentary group and the CSU. Should he buckle under the pressure without robust counter-financing in place, he will dismantle the budgetary discipline his party has long championed and capitulate to the terms set by his SPD partners.

The dilemma is compounded by factional tensions within the SPD itself. Whilst Finance Minister Klingbeil seeks to establish his ministry as a bulwark of fiscal probity, discontent simmers among the party's top brass. Persistent rumours of personal ambitions—notably from Manuela Schwesig, the minister-president of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, and Labour Minister Hubertus Heil regarding the party leadership—further constrain Mr Klingbeil's room for manoeuvre. The finance minister simply cannot afford, vis-à-vis his own party, to yield to centre-right demands for tax relief while social welfare programmes face mounting pressure to justify their costs. If the Union insists on tax cuts, the SPD will dig in on its own spending priorities—a classic recipe for continued stalemate or flight into fresh fiscal fudge.

The shuttle diplomacy of recent days underscores the gravity of the impasse. That Chancellor Merz has repeatedly had to make the pilgrimage to Munich to negotiate with Markus Söder over electricity duty, bracket creep, and the mothers' pension vividly illustrates the asymmetric distribution of power within the Union. Mr Söder operates from the position of an ostensibly unassailable Bavarian viceroy, capable of harrying the federal government without having to bear direct responsibility for the overall health of the federal budget. His dictum that one simply needs to sit down and thrash things out sounds statesmanlike, yet scarcely conceals the reality that Munich has no intention of surrendering a single euro cent of its political trophies.

At the conclusion of this trial of strength lies a fundamental home truth about contemporary German politics. The attempt to reconcile ambitious structural reforms and tangible tax relief with an expanding welfare state and particularist clientelism is running into hard economic limits. If the coalition committee merely cobbles together a shoddy compromise—whether by reshuffling shadow budgets or extending deadlines—the core problem will not have been solved, merely postponed. A federal government that purports to put Germany back on an economic footing will be judged by whether it possesses the courage to correct the misguided priorities of the past. If it fails to do so, electricity duty threatens to become the writing on the wall for a coalition trapped in the thicket of its own contradictions, testing the public's patience to breaking point.

Historical context

Electricity duty and the legacy of the ecological tax reform

Electricity duty was introduced in 1999 under the SPD-Green coalition as the centrepiece of the legislation inaugurating ecological tax reform. Its original fiscal and steering rationale was to raise the cost of energy consumption in order to stabilise statutory pension contributions and incentivise climate-friendly behaviour. Over subsequent decades, however, the levy developed into a considerable cost factor for businesses and households alike; reducing it during periods of elevated energy prices has become a perennial bone of contention in federal politics.

The coalition committee as an informal centre of power

The coalition committee has no formal constitutional footing in the Basic Law, yet since the Bonn era it has established itself as an indispensable forum for arbitration and political management. It convenes party leaders, parliamentary group chairs, and government chiefs to resolve fundamental policy impasses that can no longer be settled within the formal cabinet structure. Although its decisions lack statutory force, they place a binding political obligation on parliamentary parties and frequently shape parliamentary majorities far more decisively than ministerial bills.
